Introduction to Diffusion

From the Central Limit Theorem to the diffusion equation, through Einstein's kinetic derivation and physical examples.

Gabriel Cassoli Capellini
01 — Introduction

Introduction

In 1828, Robert Brown documented that particles in suspension move irregularly — an effect later called Brownian motion. It remained unexplained until Einstein's 1905 paper.

Building on statistical mechanics, Einstein showed the motion could be treated probabilistically: it's possible to compute the probability that a particle moves a given distance in a given time.

1.1 — The intuition behind diffusion

The intuition behind diffusion

During Brownian motion, particles tend to spread until they are distributed ever more uniformly through the medium. If a region I currently holds twice as many particles as a region II, the expected number of particles migrating from I to II will be twice as large as the reverse — not because any single particle behaves differently, but simply because twice as many of them are making the same random attempt.

This is the essence of diffusion, and it's intrinsically tied to Brownian motion — the idea we'll turn into an equation over the next few sections.

1.3 — Why a Gaussian, and what's ahead

Why a Gaussian, and what's ahead

One of Einstein's results is that the probability density of a particle's displacement takes the shape of a Gaussian curve — a direct consequence of the Central Limit Theorem: the particle's final position is the sum of many independent, identically distributed random "kicks" from surrounding molecules.

These notes follow that logic: first the statistics behind the Gaussian, then Einstein's derivation of the diffusion equation itself, and finally a few physical applications — worked through with live simulations.

02 — Why a Gaussian?

Why a Gaussian?

Before writing down the diffusion equation, let's understand why the Gaussian shows up everywhere — starting from the Moment Generating Function.

2.1 — Moment Generating Function

What is a Moment Generating Function?

The Moment Generating Function (MGF) is a function that returns the expected value of \(e^{tX}\). For a discrete random variable, the MGF is a sum: \[ M_X(t) = \sum e^{tX}\,P(X=x). \]

To obtain the n-th moment (\(E[X^n]\)), we take the n-th derivative of the MGF with respect to \(t\), evaluated at \(t=0\): \[ E[X^n] = M_X^{(n)}(0). \]

2.2 — Proof

Proof

We now show that the standardized sum of i.i.d. random variables converges to a Gaussian as \(n\to\infty\).

Consider a data set \(X_i\), \(i=1,\dots,n\), independent and identically distributed. For simplicity, assume the mean is zero and the variance \(\sigma^2\) equals \(1\). The sum of the data can be written as \[ S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i. \] Since the data come from the same distribution, their MGF \(m(t)\) is the same.

Property: the MGF of a sum is the product of the MGFs.

Thus, the MGF of \(S_n\) is \([m(t)]^n\).

2.2 — Proof

Let's now take the Taylor expansion of \(m(t)\): \[ m(t) = m(0) + t\,m'(0) + \frac{t^2}{2}m''(0) + O(t^3). \]

Since \(m(0)=1\), the first derivative of the MGF is the mean (zero here), and the second derivative is the variance (\(1\) here), so: \[ m(t) = 1 + \frac{t^2\sigma^2}{2} + O(t^3). \]

2.2 — Proof
Property: if \(Y = bX\), then \(m_Y(t) = m_X(bt)\).

Using this property, define \(Z = S_n/(\sigma\sqrt{n})\), so that: \[ m_Z(t) = m_S\!\left(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right) = \left[m\!\left(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n. \]

2.2 — Proof

Expanding \(m(t/\sigma\sqrt{n})\): \[ m\!\left(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{\sigma^2 n} + O\!\left(\frac{t^3}{\sigma^3 n^{3/2}}\right) = 1+\frac{t^2}{2n}. \]

Notice that in the higher-order terms the power of \(n\) grows increasingly large — their contribution vanishes as \(n\to\infty\).

2.2 — Proof

Let's now take the limit \(n\to\infty\) of \(m_Z(t)\): \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} m_Z(t) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left[1+\frac{t^2}{2n}+\epsilon^3\right]^n = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{t^2}{2n}\right)^n. \]

From the definition of the exponential, \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\alpha/n\right)^n = e^{\alpha}\), so: \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{t^2}{2n}\right)^n = e^{t^2/2}, \] which is the MGF of the standard normal distribution \(N(0,1)\).

This is the Central Limit Theorem.

03 — Einstein's Description

Einstein's Description

We now derive the diffusion equation from first principles, following Einstein's 1905 argument: particles move independently, each taking a small step during a short time interval τ.

3.1 — The step distribution φ(μ)

The step distribution φ(μ)

Suppose $N$ particles are suspended in the liquid. During each interval $\tau$, a particle moves a distance $\Delta x=\mu$. The number of particles taking a step between $\mu$ and $\mu+d\mu$ is \[ dN = N\,\phi(\mu)\,d\mu. \]

$\phi(\mu)$ is a probability density, so it must be normalized, $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\phi(\mu)\,d\mu=1$, and by symmetry it is even, $\phi(\mu)=\phi(-\mu)$ — no direction is preferred.

3.2 — η(x,t) and the question

η(x,t) and the question we want to answer

Let $\eta(x,t)$ be the number of particles per unit length around position $x$, at time $t$. Unlike $\phi$, $\eta$ is not normalized to $1$ — integrating it over all space gives $N$.

Knowing $\eta(x,t)$, what is the concentration after one more step $\tau$ has elapsed?

3.3 — Building the equation

Building the equation

A particle arriving at $x$ at time $t+\tau$ started somewhere at $x+\mu$ at time $t$, and made exactly the jump needed to land at $x$. Summing this over every possible origin $\mu$:

\[ \eta(x,t+\tau) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \eta(x+\mu,t)\,\phi(\mu)\,d\mu \]

3.4 — Two Taylor expansions

Two Taylor expansions

$\tau$ is small: expand in time, holding $x$ fixed, to second order: \[ \eta(x,t+\tau)\approx \eta(x,t)+\tau\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}+\frac{\tau^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial t^2}. \]

$\mu$ is also small: expand in space, holding $t$ fixed, to the same order: \[ \eta(x+\mu,t)\approx \eta(x,t)+\mu\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}+\frac{\mu^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial x^2}. \]

3.4 — Substituting into the integral

Substituting into the integral

Plug both expansions into $\eta(x,t+\tau)=\int \eta(x+\mu,t)\,\phi(\mu)\,d\mu$. Since $\eta(x,t)$ and its $x$-derivatives don't depend on $\mu$, they come out of the integral:

\[ \eta+\tau\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}+\frac{\tau^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial t^2} = \eta\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\phi(\mu)\,d\mu + \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\mu\,\phi(\mu)\,d\mu + \frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial x^2}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi(\mu)\,d\mu \]

Three integrals remain on the right — each one multiplying $\phi(\mu)$ by a different power of $\mu$.

3.4 — Why the middle term vanishes

Why the middle term vanishes

Look at $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mu\,\phi(\mu)\,d\mu$. Since $\mu$ is odd and $\phi(\mu)$ is even, their product $\mu\,\phi(\mu)$ is odd — and it's being integrated over the symmetric interval $(-\infty,+\infty)$.

Property: the integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is zero.

So the whole middle term vanishes: $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mu\,\phi(\mu)\,d\mu=0$. This is exactly where "no preferred direction" ($\phi$ even) does its work.

3.5 — The diffusion equation

Assembling the diffusion equation

What's left: the first integral is $1$ by normalization, the middle term is gone, and the last integral is what we'll call $D$. That gives the generalized diffusion equation \[ \frac{\tau}{2}\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial t^2}+\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=D\,\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial x^2}, \qquad D=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi(\mu)\,d\mu. \]

In the limit τ∂²η/∂t² ≪ ∂η/∂t, this reduces to Einstein's diffusion equation.

\[ \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 \eta}{\partial x^2} \]

04 — Applications

Applications of the Diffusion Problem

Three very different physical settings, governed by the same diffusion equation, solved with the same tools — only the initial condition changes.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

The Perfume Case

Consider a long, narrow room — narrow enough to treat as a line, a one-dimensional problem. At the origin, someone opens a bottle of perfume (or drops a bit of ink into a thin tube of water). At $t=0$, all $N$ molecules sit at that single point.

The question: what is the concentration $\eta(x,t)$ at every point in the room, at every later instant?

4.3 — The Perfume Case

The initial condition

All particles at one point, at $t=0$, is precisely a Dirac delta: \[ \eta(x,0)=N\delta(x). \]

Consistent with $\eta$ integrating to $N$: $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}N\delta(x)\,dx=N$.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

Why Fourier, and not a guess?

Most textbooks simply state the Gaussian solution and check that it works, as if it had been guessed. Instead, notice that $e^{ikx}$ is an eigenfunction of $\partial^2/\partial x^2$: \[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}e^{ikx}=-k^2 e^{ikx}. \]

Decomposing $\eta$ into plane waves turns the PDE — which couples every $x$ together — into independent ODEs, one per mode $k$. That's why Fourier is the natural tool here, not a trick.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

Separation of variables

Assume the solution factors as $\eta(x,t)=\omega(x)\,\gamma(t)$ and substitute into the diffusion equation: \[ \omega(x)\,\gamma'(t) = D\,\omega''(x)\,\gamma(t). \]

Dividing both sides by $\omega(x)\gamma(t)$ separates the two variables completely: \[ \frac{\gamma'(t)}{\gamma(t)} = D\,\frac{\omega''(x)}{\omega(x)}. \]

4.3 — The Perfume Case

Same constant on both sides

The left side depends only on $t$; the right side depends only on $x$. For the equality to hold at every $(x,t)$, both sides must equal the same constant — call it $-Dk^2$: \[ \frac{\gamma'}{\gamma} = -Dk^2, \qquad \frac{\omega''}{\omega} = -k^2. \]

Two ordinary differential equations, one in $t$ and one in $x$ — exactly the simplification we wanted from the PDE.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

From separation to Fourier

Solving each equation: $\omega''=-k^2\omega$ gives $\omega(x)=e^{ikx}$ — precisely the eigenfunction condition from before, arrived at a different way. And $\gamma'=-Dk^2\gamma$ gives $\gamma(t)=e^{-Dk^2t}$.

This works for any $k$, giving one solution $e^{ikx}e^{-Dk^2t}$ per value of $k$. Since the equation is linear, the general solution is a superposition of all of them: \[ \eta(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \eta_k(t)\,e^{ikx}\,dk, \qquad \eta_k(t)=\eta_k(0)\,e^{-Dk^2t}. \]

That's the Fourier representation we've been using — separation of variables applied to every $k$ at once.

Physically, $\gamma(t)=e^{-Dk^2t}$ tells us large $k$ (rapid spatial oscillation) decays fastest — exactly what smoothing should look like.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

Applying the initial condition

$\eta_k(0)$ comes from transforming $\eta(x,0)$ itself: \[ \eta_k(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \eta(x,0)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx. \]

For our delta, $\eta(x,0)=N\delta(x)$, so the integral collapses to a single evaluation at $x=0$: \[ \eta_k(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} N\delta(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx = \frac{N}{\sqrt{2\pi}}. \]

Flat in $k$ — a delta is the "sharpest" object there is, so it contains every spatial frequency in equal amount.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

The full mode solution

Combining $\eta_k(0)=N/\sqrt{2\pi}$ with the time-dependence $\eta_k(t)=\eta_k(0)e^{-Dk^2t}$ found earlier:

\[ \eta_k(t) = \frac{N}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-Dk^2t}. \]

Every mode is now known, at every time. What remains is purely mechanical: undo the transform to get back to $\eta(x,t)$.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

Back to real space

By definition, $\eta(x,t)$ is recovered from its modes by the inverse transform: \[ \eta(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \eta_k(t)\,e^{ikx}\,dk. \]

Substituting $\eta_k(t)$ from the previous slide: \[ \eta(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{N}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-Dk^2t}\,e^{ikx}\,dk = \frac{N}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-Dtk^2+ixk}\,dk. \]

One integral left to compute — and it's a standard one.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

Evaluating the Gaussian integral

The integral has the standard form $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-ak^2+ibk}\,dk=\sqrt{\pi/a}\;e^{-b^2/4a}$, with $a=Dt$ and $b=x$:

\[ \eta(x,t) = \frac{N}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{Dt}}\;e^{-x^2/4Dt} = \frac{N}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\;e^{-x^2/4Dt}. \]

This is the closed-form answer — no more transforms, no more integrals left to do.

4.3 — The Perfume Case

A delta becomes a Gaussian

A delta at $t=0$ evolves into a Gaussian that widens as $t$ grows — exactly the shape we'll watch form in the simulation.

4.4 — The Rio Tietê Case

The Rio Tietê Case

Now imagine a river, also treated as one-dimensional. A pollutant is dumped uniformly along the entire left bank, giving a step initial condition:

\[ \eta(x,0) = \begin{cases}C, & x<0\\0,& x>0\end{cases} \]

4.4 — The Rio Tietê Case

The diffusion equation, once more

Whatever the initial condition, the concentration still obeys the same equation we derived earlier from Einstein's argument:

\[ \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 \eta}{\partial x^2} \]

The only thing that changes from problem to problem is $\eta(x,0)$. Is there a way to solve for any initial condition, once and for all?

4.4 — The Rio Tietê Case

Since the equation is linear...

The diffusion equation is linear: if $\eta_1$ and $\eta_2$ are both solutions, so is any combination $a\eta_1+b\eta_2$. That suggests we could build the step's solution out of solutions we already know, superposed together.

This idea — solve a linear equation once for a point source, then superpose — has a name: the Green's function. But what exactly is a Green's function?

4.4 — What is a Green's function?

What is a Green's function?

Given a linear differential operator $L$ acting on functions over a domain $\Omega$, the Green's function $G=G(y,s)$ at the point $s\in\Omega$ is any solution of \[ L\,G(y,s) = \delta(y-s). \]

In words: $G(y,s)$ is the system's response, measured at $y$, to a unit point impulse placed at $s$.

4.4 — Why bother? Superposition

Why bother? Superposition

Multiply both sides by an arbitrary function $f(s)$ and integrate over $s$: \[ \int L\,G(y,s)\,f(s)\,ds = \int \delta(y-s)\,f(s)\,ds = f(y). \]

Since $L$ acts on $y$, not $s$, it can be pulled outside the integral on the left: $L\left[\int G(y,s)\,f(s)\,ds\right] = f(y)$. So for the equation $Lu(y)=f(y)$, the solution is \[ u(y) = \int G(y,s)\,f(s)\,ds. \]

Solve once, for a point impulse — and you've solved it for every source $f$, for free.

4.4 — No source, just an initial profile

The homogeneous case, with initial data

Now suppose there's no source at all ($f=0$), just a homogeneous equation $Lu=0$ with a nonzero initial profile $u(x,0)=u_0(x)$. Writing $y=(x,t)$ and $s=(\xi,\tau)$ for space-time points, our operator is \[ L = \partial_t - D\,\partial_x^2. \]

To handle this case we need the adjoint operator $L^*$, defined so that $\int\!\!\int\big[v\,Lu - u\,L^*v\big]\,d\xi\,d\tau$ equals only boundary terms. Integrating by parts once in $\tau$ (flipping a sign) and twice in $\xi$ (no sign flip) gives \[ L^*v = -\partial_\tau v - D\,\partial_\xi^2 v. \]

4.4 — Opening the boundary terms

Opening the boundary terms

Define $G(y,s)$ by $L^*_sG(y,s)=\delta(y-s)$, and plug $v=G(y,s)$, $u=\eta(s)$ into the identity, integrating $\xi$ over the line and $\tau$ from $0$ to $t$. Since $L\eta=0$ and $L^*_sG=\delta(y-s)$, the left side collapses to $-\eta(x,t)$.

The right side splits into two pieces: a temporal boundary term at $\tau=0$ and $\tau=t$ (from integrating by parts in $\tau$), and a spatial term at $\xi\to\pm\infty$ (from the double integration by parts in $\xi$). The spatial term vanishes — both $\eta$ and $G$ decay at infinity — leaving \[ -\eta(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\Big[G(y,(\xi,t))\,\eta(\xi,t) - G(y,(\xi,0))\,\eta(\xi,0)\Big]\,d\xi. \]

4.4 — Causality: only t=0 survives

Causality: only t=0 survives

The $\tau=t$ term vanishes by definition of the retarded Green's function: effects can't precede causes, so $G(y,(\xi,t))\equiv0$ at the observation instant itself — consistent with the Gaussian only "switching on" in the limit $\tau\to t^-$.

What's left is exactly the propagation formula we wanted: \[ \eta(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} G(y,(\xi,0))\,u_0(\xi)\,d\xi. \]

4.4 — What is a Green's function?

The Green's function of our diffusion equation

We already solved for the response to a unit point source at the origin — that's exactly the perfume problem with $N=1$: \[ G(x,t;0,0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,e^{-x^2/4Dt}. \]

Since $D$ is constant, the problem is invariant under translations in space and time: $G(x,t;\xi,\tau)=G(x-\xi,\,t-\tau)$. Setting $\tau=0$ gives exactly the propagator we need: \[ G(x-x',t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,e^{-(x-x')^2/4Dt}. \]

4.4 — Solving by superposition

Solving the Rio Tietê by superposition

Any initial condition is a sum of point sources, each weighted by how much concentration was actually there: \[ \eta(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\eta(x',0)\,G(x-x',t)\,dx'. \]

For the step, only $x'<0$ contributes, where $\eta(x',0)=C$: \[ \eta(x,t) = C\int_{-\infty}^{0} G(x-x',t)\,dx'. \]

4.4 — The Rio Tietê Case

Solving for the step

Only $x'<0$ contributes. Substituting $w=(x-x')/\sqrt{4Dt}$ turns the integral into the complementary error function: \[ \eta(x,t) = \frac{C}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right) = \frac{C}{2}\left[1-\mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right)\right]. \]

4.4 — The Rio Tietê Case

Does it make sense?

$t\to 0$: $\mathrm{erf}(\mp\infty)=\mp1$, recovering the sharp step ($C$ for $x<0$, $0$ for $x>0$). $t\to\infty$: $\mathrm{erf}(0)=0$, so $\eta\to C/2$ everywhere — full mixing.

Same diffusion equation, same Green's function, a very different-looking result: a step smooths into an S-curve instead of a peak spreading outward.

4.5 — The Exponential Case

A Third Case: Exponential Decay

A quick conceptual note first. Linearity means $a\eta_1+b\eta_2$ is a solution whenever $\eta_1,\eta_2$ are — but that does not mean every new solution is a finite combination of just the Gaussian and the erfc we've found so far. What actually guarantees it is stronger: any $\eta_0(x)$ is a continuous superposition of deltas, so by linearity its solution is the same superposition of $G(x-x',t)$'s. $G$ is the building block — the Gaussian and the erfc are themselves already superpositions of it.

Consider a pollutant dumped only on the bank $x>0$, already decaying exponentially with distance: \[ \eta(x,0)=\eta_0(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-x}, & x>0 \\ 0, & x<0. \end{cases} \]

4.5 — The Exponential Case

Setting up the convolution

Same recipe as always — plug $\eta_0$ into the propagation formula, integrating only over $x'>0$ where $\eta_0(x')=e^{-x'}$: \[ \eta(x,t) = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-x'}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,e^{-(x-x')^2/4Dt}\,dx'. \]

The trick, as always with these Gaussian integrals: combine both exponents and complete the square in $x'$.

4.5 — The Exponential Case

Completing the square

Combining the two exponents and completing the square in $x'$: \[ -x' - \frac{(x-x')^2}{4Dt} = (Dt-x) \;-\; \frac{\big[x'-(x-2Dt)\big]^2}{4Dt}. \]

The part that only depends on $x$ and $t$ pulls out of the integral; what's left inside is a plain Gaussian centered at $x-2Dt$.

4.5 — The Exponential Case

The result

Substituting $w=[x'-(x-2Dt)]/\sqrt{4Dt}$ turns the remaining integral into an $\mathrm{erfc}$, exactly as in the Rio Tietê case: \[ \eta(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\,e^{\,Dt-x}\;\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{2Dt-x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right). \]

4.5 — The Exponential Case

Does it recover the initial condition?

As $t\to0^+$: for $x>0$ the erfc argument goes to $-\infty$, so $\mathrm{erfc}\to2$ and $\eta\to e^{-x}$; for $x<0$ it goes to $+\infty$, so $\mathrm{erfc}\to0$ and $\eta\to0$. Both limits match $\eta_0(x)$ exactly.

Notice the pattern across all three cases: the same $G(x-x',t)$ sits inside every integral — only the support and weight $\eta_0(x')$ change. That universality of $G$, not a coincidence of final forms, is what makes the Green's-function approach worth the setup.

4.6 — Estimating Avogadro's Number

Estimating Avogadro's Number

One of the most interesting applications of Einstein's approach is estimating Avogadro's number directly from the statistics of Brownian motion.

Dividing $\eta(x,t)$ by the total number of particles $N$ gives the probability of finding a particle between $x$ and $x+dx$, given that it started at the origin: \[ P(x,t) = \frac{\eta(x,t)}{N} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,e^{-x^2/4Dt}. \]

A Gaussian with mean $\langle x\rangle=0$ and variance $\sigma^2=2Dt$.

4.6 — Estimating Avogadro's Number

Mean squared displacement

Computing the mean squared displacement directly from $P(x,t)$: \[ \langle x^2\rangle = \frac{2}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\int_0^{\infty} x^2\,e^{-x^2/4Dt}\,dx = 2Dt. \]

Known as Einstein's relation — the average squared distance a particle wanders grows linearly with time, not with time squared as ballistic motion would predict.

4.6 — Estimating Avogadro's Number

From D to Avogadro's number

For spherical particles of radius $a$, Stokes' law gives the diffusion coefficient: \[ D = \frac{k_B T}{6\pi\gamma a}. \]

Substituting into the mean squared displacement: \[ \langle x^2\rangle = \frac{RT}{3\pi N_a\,\gamma a}\,t \;\propto\; t. \]

Since $\langle x^2\rangle$, $t$, $\gamma$, and $a$ are all directly measurable — with a stopwatch and a microscope, as Perrin did — this expression lets us solve for $N_a$: a statistical argument about random walks turned into one of the first direct measurements of Avogadro's number.
